Measuring quality

30% → 80%conversation CSAT
1M+calls a week in scope
Every releasegated on the score

My role: designed and launched the evaluation framework, and made it a release gate.

Trigger

Customers judge a voice agent by the worst call they heard that week, then they ring you. Complaints were arriving and we could not say whether the last release had made calls better or worse.

Problem

A new model, prompt, voice or telephony route could ship with nothing checking the result. With no shared definition of a good call, every quality debate was an argument about the most recent complaint, and sales could only promise that the agent was good.

Solution

  • Wrote down what a good call is, as a scoring rubric.
  • Scored real production calls, first by people and then by a model once it agreed with the people often enough to be trusted.
  • Kept a fixed comparison set so scores stayed comparable month to month.
  • Put the score in front of releases: a change that lowered it did not ship.

Key decision

Make the score a release gate rather than a dashboard. That costs speed. A score nobody is allowed to ignore was worth more than a faster release train.

Result

Conversation CSAT went from 30% to 80%. The baseline came from a thinner instrument than the 80%, so part of the gain is better measurement as well as a better product. Sales started running prospects’ own call recordings through the rubric before signature, which turned quality from a promise into a specification.

What I’d do differently

Fix the measurement before fixing the product. Because the baseline came from a thinner instrument, I cannot say exactly how much of the 50-point gain was the product.

Opening enterprise

3 → 105enterprise customers
3xARR over the same period
100k min/wkflagship account unblocked
2first-to-market integrations

My role: prioritised and shipped the platform capabilities that opened the enterprise segment, and led both integrations.

Trigger

Enterprise deals were not being lost in the demo. They were stalling afterwards.

Problem

The product was bought bottom-up: a team could sign up in an afternoon without talking to us. Enterprise buyers needed things that have nothing to do with how well the agent talks: single sign-on, roles, audit logs, data controls, concurrency limits, a security questionnaire. Others needed the agent to work where their calls already lived. Any one of these could stop a deal.

Solution

  • Went through the stalled deals and ranked blockers by the pipeline behind them, not by how often they came up, then shipped in that order.
  • Became the first vendor to integrate Zoom Phone with agent AI, which unblocked a flagship customer doing 100,000 minutes a week.
  • Moved early when WhatsApp opened up phone calling and became the first voice AI provider on it, opening Latin America.

Key decision

Rank by pipeline value, not by how loudly a request was made. That meant shipping procurement basics ahead of work that was more interesting to build, in the order deals were dying.

Result

Enterprise customers grew from 3 to 105 and ARR grew 3x over the same period. Those are company numbers: product removed the reasons deals stopped, and sales closed them.

What I’d do differently

We had built for the first meeting and not the second. I would start the procurement checklist when the first enterprise deal enters the pipeline, not when it stalls.

Synthflow · Senior PM, then Head of Product · 2025 to now

Making agents predictable

My role: framed the problem for engineering and co-designed Flow Designer with them.

Trigger

Enterprise buyers noticed that the same input could take a different path through the same agent.

Problem

In a demo, that reads as flexibility. In a customer’s real workflow, it reads as a bug, and it made agents hard to trust with processes that have to happen the same way every time. Both obvious fixes fail: a longer prompt is still hoping, and hard-coding every path rebuilds the phone tree the customer wanted to escape.

Solution

Flow Designer, a hybrid deterministic system. The steps that must happen in order happen in order, and the model handles the conversation in between.

Key decision

Treat determinism as a product decision, not a model setting: decide which parts of a call the customer needs identical every time, and only fix those. Everything else stays conversational.

Result

Agents that take the same path twice, which is also what makes them measurable against the quality rubric above.

What I’d do differently

Build determinism and quality measurement as one project. They are the same question asked from two sides: did the agent do what it was supposed to?

Founding Lua Health

My role: CEO and the first product manager. I owned vision, roadmap, discovery, the first release, hiring and fundraising.

Trigger

A university spinout built on clinical research into measuring mental wellbeing.

Problem

The standard way to measure how a workforce was doing was another annual survey, and nobody wanted another one.

Solution

I wrote the first spec, ran discovery with the people who buy wellbeing software, shipped the first version, hired the first engineers and PMs, and raised EUR 1M.

Key decision

Sell to the people who buy wellbeing software, not to the employees whose wellbeing was being measured. It was where the budget was, and it is also where the product’s core tension came from.

Result

EUR 1M raised, national and international recognition, and a company that wound down in 2024.

What I’d do differently

Test the buyer–user gap before building. The person holding the budget is rarely the person with the problem, and the person with the problem rarely wants to be measured. I learned more about the distance between a good thesis and a paying customer in those three years than in the five before them.
