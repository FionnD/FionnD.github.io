Synthflow · Head of Product · 2025 to now

Measuring quality

Voice AI is easy to demo and hard to measure. At a million calls a week any single call can sound excellent while plenty of others are going badly, and customers do not experience the average. They experience the worst call they heard that week, and then they ring you.

We did not have a reliable way to tell whether a change had made things better. A model change, a prompt change, a new voice, a new telephony route: all of it could ship without anything checking the result. Which leaves an argument about quality as an argument about the most recent complaint.

The first thing we built was a definition of good, written down.

Calls were scored by a person first and by a model later, once the model agreed with the people often enough to be trusted. The calls came from production rather than from demos, and we kept a fixed set so the numbers stayed comparable from one month to the next.

Then we put the whole thing in front of releases. If a change made the score worse, it did not go out.

Conversation CSAT went from 30% to 80%. I am careful with that number. The 30% was measured with a thinner instrument than the 80%, so some of the improvement is the product and some of it is the measurement.

The commercial change was less visible than the dashboard. Sales could put a prospect's own call recordings through the rubric before signing anything, which is a different conversation from promising that the agent is good. Quality became something we could specify.

Synthflow · Head of Product · 2025 to now

Selling to enterprise

The product was bought from the bottom up. A team could sign up in an afternoon and get to value before talking to anyone at the company.

Enterprise procurement then asks for things that have nothing to do with how well the agent talks. Single sign-on, roles, audit logs, data controls, concurrency limits, and a security questionnaire somebody has to answer. None of it is the AI, and any of it can stop a deal.

We had built for the first meeting and not the second. So I went through the stalled deals and ranked the blockers by the pipeline behind them rather than by how often they came up in conversation.

We shipped the ones that unblocked the most pipeline first, in the order the deals were dying. That was not the order that was most interesting to build.

Enterprise customers went from 3 to 105, and ARR grew 3x over the same period. Those are company numbers. Product removed the reasons deals stopped. Sales did the rest.

Lua Health · Co-founder and CEO · 2021 to 2024

Founding Lua Health

Lua Health was a university spinout. The idea was to measure how a workforce was doing without sending everybody another annual survey.

I was CEO. I wrote the first spec, ran discovery with the people who buy wellbeing software, shipped the first version, hired the first engineers and PMs, and raised EUR 1M.

The person holding the budget is rarely the person with the problem, and the person with the problem rarely wants to be measured. That is a difficult pair of facts to build a product on.

We got Business Post 30 Under 30 and Slush Top 20. Both are good for morale and do not pay salaries.

The company wound down in 2024.

I learned more in those three years than in the five before them, mostly about how far a good thesis is from a paying customer.
