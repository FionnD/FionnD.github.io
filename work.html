Synthflow · Senior PM, then Head of Product · 2025 to now

Measuring quality

Voice AI is easy to demo and hard to measure. At a million calls a week, any single call can sound excellent while plenty of others go badly. Customers don’t experience the average. They experience the worst call they heard that week, and then they ring you.

We had no reliable way to tell whether a change made things better. A new model, a new prompt, a new voice, a new telephony route: any of it could ship without anything checking the result. So every argument about quality was really an argument about the most recent complaint.

The first thing we built was a written definition of a good call.

Calls were scored by a person first and by a model later, once the model agreed with the people often enough to be trusted. The calls came from production rather than from demos, and we kept a fixed set so the numbers stayed comparable from one month to the next.

Then we put the whole thing in front of releases. If a change made the score worse, it did not go out.

Conversation CSAT went from 30% to 80%. I hold that number loosely: the 30% came from a thinner instrument than the 80%, so part of the gain is the product and part is the measurement.

The bigger change was commercial. Sales could run a prospect’s own call recordings through the rubric before anyone signed, which is a different conversation from promising the agent is good. Quality stopped being a claim and became something we could specify.

Synthflow · Senior PM, then Head of Product · 2025 to now

Making agents predictable

A language model will happily take a different route through the same conversation twice. In a demo that reads as flexibility. In a customer’s refund process it reads as a bug, and enterprise buyers noticed before we had a good answer for them.

The tempting fixes both fail. Write a longer prompt and you are still hoping. Hard-code every path and you have rebuilt the phone tree the customer was trying to get away from.

I framed the problem for engineering as a product question rather than a model one: which parts of a call does the customer need to be identical every time, and which parts are allowed to sound human? We designed the answer together as Flow Designer, a hybrid deterministic system. The steps that have to happen in order happen in order, and the model handles the conversation in between.

It is the same problem as measuring quality from the other side. You cannot score an agent against a definition of good if it will not take the same path twice.

Synthflow · Senior PM, then Head of Product · 2025 to now

Selling to enterprise

The product was built to be bought from the bottom up. A team could sign up in an afternoon and get value before talking to anyone at the company.

Enterprise procurement asks for things that have nothing to do with how well the agent talks: single sign-on, roles, audit logs, data controls, concurrency limits, and a security questionnaire somebody has to answer. None of it is the AI, and any of it can stop a deal.

We had built for the first meeting and not the second. So I went through the stalled deals and ranked the blockers by the pipeline behind them rather than by how often they came up in conversation.

We shipped in the order deals were dying, not the order that was most interesting to build.

Some blockers were not procurement at all but where the calls already lived. A flagship customer doing 100,000 minutes a week ran its phones on Zoom, so we became the first vendor to integrate Zoom’s agent AI, which unblocked the account. When WhatsApp opened up phone calling, we moved early and were the first voice AI provider on it, which opened Latin America, where a lot of customer conversations already happen on WhatsApp.

Enterprise customers went from 3 to 105, and ARR grew 3x over the same period. Product removed the reasons deals stopped. Sales did the rest.

Lua Health · Co-founder and CEO · 2021 to 2024

Founding Lua Health

Lua Health was a university spinout. The idea was to measure how a workforce was doing without sending everybody another annual survey.

As CEO I was also the first product manager. I wrote the first spec, ran discovery with the people who buy wellbeing software, shipped the first version, hired the first engineers and PMs, and raised EUR 1M.

The person holding the budget is rarely the person with the problem, and the person with the problem rarely wants to be measured. That is a hard pair of facts to build a product on.

We were named in Business Post’s 30 Under 30 and Slush’s Top 20. Good for morale; neither pays salaries.

The company wound down in 2024.

I learned more in those three years than in the five before them, mostly about the distance between a good thesis and a paying customer.
