Ship the MCP. Listen to What It Asks For.

Capture what the agent is trying to do, as well as which tools it calls.

Assuming you already have a usable API, I would start with an MCP server.

Customers can use your product through an existing assistant. You get to learn what they actually want before spending three months building a copilot around your best guesses.

Instrument it with PostHog before launch. Capture tool calls, errors, and the intent behind the request. Someone investigating a failed call and someone preparing a weekly report might fetch the same transcripts. What they need next is quite different.

At Synthflow, we found most MCP usage was for analysing calls. That gives you something specific to build around. “Customers want AI” mostly gives you meetings.

On 21 October 2026, PostHog’s public dashboard reported agents explaining why they were calling a tool 92% of the time. That gives you something much more useful than a list of method names.

The agent can explain what it is attempting while it attempts it. You can compare that explanation with the tools it chooses and where it gets stuck.

It is still the agent’s account of the task. Treat it as evidence to investigate, rather than a perfectly transcribed customer need.

Errors are part of that evidence. A failed request might expose a bug, a confusing tool description, or a perfectly reasonable task your API cannot currently support.

Repeated calls might mean heavy usage. They might also mean the assistant has spent five minutes walking into the same door.

Look at the sequence. What was it trying to achieve? What information was missing? Did the customer finish the job?

And remember that a tool you never built cannot appear in your successful-call dashboard. Capture unsupported requests where possible and follow up with users.

Your first MCP release should help you decide what to build next. Logging that someone called get_calls is a fairly modest start.
